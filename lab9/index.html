The statistical file is the IMF World Economic Outlook nominal-GDP series (NGDPD, current prices) for 2025. It is limited to the 50 largest economies. Country polygons come from Natural Earth and are stored as GeoJSON with iso3 and name. The two files are joined on the ISO-3 code. Economies outside this top-50 list stay on the choropleth as missing data. They are never colored or sized as if GDP were zero. The IMF notes that WEO observations may include staff estimates where complete official data are unavailable and can later be revised.

2. 2025 GDP Choropleth

Country shape is the real boundary. Color is 2025 nominal GDP.

Log color scale

Hover or click a country to highlight it in both maps.

Visual Encoding Guide

Position: geographic location after a Natural Earth projection.

Shape: actual country boundary from GeoJSON, drawn with d3.geoPath().

Color: 2025 GDP on a logarithmic sequential blue scale.

Missing data: neutral gray. These countries are outside the top-50 file, not zero-GDP countries.

Interaction: tooltip, highlight, and zoom/pan. The same country is also highlighted on the cartogram.

GDP in this set runs from about $303 billion to more than $30 trillion. A linear color scale would isolate the United States and China and leave almost every other economy in the same pale bin. I used d3.scaleSequentialLog with d3.interpolateBlues so Germany, India, Brazil, and the smaller top-50 economies remain distinguishable. The log transform is a design choice for readability, not a claim that GDP itself is logarithmic.

3. 2025 GDP Cartogram

Country area represents 2025 GDP. Larger GDP occupies more visual area.

Area encodes GDP

Visual Encoding Guide

Area: 2025 GDP. This is the primary encoding. A country’s visual size is no longer its land area.

Color: the same logarithmic GDP scale as the choropleth, so the two views stay comparable.

Shape: a contiguous cartogram. Shared borders stay attached and the whole landmass is stretched like a rubber sheet until area matches GDP. This is the Worldmapper-style map, computed with the Dougenik–Chrisman–Niemeyer algorithm in topogram.

Position: still a world map. Neighbors remain neighbors; continents do not float apart.

Missing data: countries outside the top 50 stay on the map as gray connecting tissue so the topology does not tear. They receive only a small residual area for that purpose. They are not colored or sized as if GDP were zero.

Interaction: tooltip and linked highlighting with the choropleth.

Read area first. The United States and China should swell in place. Western Europe should puff up. Russia, Canada, and Australia should thin out while remaining attached to their neighbors. Hover a warped shape if it is hard to recognize; the choropleth will mark the same ISO-3 feature.

4. Design and Comparison

Task, encoding, geographic information, and the trade-off between the two maps.

The choropleth keeps real coastlines, so Russia, Canada, China, Brazil, and Australia look large before color is read. Color then marks the United States and China as the two darkest economies; Germany, Japan, the United Kingdom, and India form the next visible tier. Much of Africa and Central Asia stays the missing-data gray, which is not zero. The cartogram is a contiguous rubber-sheet map: neighbors still touch, but area now follows 2025 GDP. The United States and China swell in place; Western Europe puffs up; Russia, Canada, and Australia shrink into thinner shapes. Compact high-output economies such as Japan, South Korea, and the Low Countries gain area without leaving their region. Gray countries remain only as connecting tissue so the landmass stays one piece; they are not sized as zero GDP. The choropleth is the better tool for location, borders, and regional clusters. The cartogram is the better tool for magnitude: which economies are largest, and by how much. Switching from color to area makes the U.S.–China gap hard to miss. A log color scale keeps mid-sized countries readable but compresses that gap; area does not.
